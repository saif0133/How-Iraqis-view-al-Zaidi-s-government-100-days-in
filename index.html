The first 100 days

What has actually changed, and what does it tell us about the road ahead?

One hundred days. In the life of a country, it is barely a moment. But in politics, it has become the first real test of whether a new government can turn promises into progress.

When Ali al-Zaidi was sworn in as Iraq’s prime minister on May 14, he arrived under unusual circumstances. A businessman with no previous government or political post, he was chosen as a consensus candidate after months of deadlock. He took office with an incomplete cabinet: parliament approved 14 ministers while nine ministries, including Interior and Defence, were still pending.

From his first day, the government faced overlapping pressures: oil exports, liquidity and public salaries, bringing weapons under state control, corruption, a deepening water and drought crisis, and a tense regional environment.

The government says it has already opened files that had been delayed for years. It points to recovered public funds and gold, a new four-year government program, and reforms in finance and electricity. Critics argue that many of these decisions have not yet translated into tangible changes in daily life.

So the central question is not simply whether the government has acted. It is whether Iraqis believe those actions are producing results.
01 · The opening mandate

A strong start begins with al-Zaidi himself.

The clearest finding in the survey is the prime minister’s relatively strong personal opening position. Seventy-seven percent say they are somewhat or fully satisfied with his selection, while 75 percent say he has borne the responsibilities of office to a great or moderate extent.

Those numbers do not tell us whether the government has solved Iraq’s structural problems. They tell us something more immediate: after a difficult formation process, al-Zaidi begins with substantial public room to act. The charts below show that mandate from three angles, his selection, his overall evaluation, and whether Iraqis believe he has carried the weight of the office.

02 · Beyond the prime minister

Personal approval is not the same thing as confidence in the political system.

A new prime minister does not govern alone. The presidency, cabinet, parliament, its speaker, and the judiciary all shape whether political decisions can be turned into durable policy.

That makes the institutional ratings especially important. Read together, they show whether the early confidence surrounding al-Zaidi extends to the wider state, or whether the public is separating its view of the prime minister from its view of the institutions around him.

03 · From approval to delivery

The harder test is what people can actually feel in daily life.

The government says its first months produced a 2026 to 2029 program, new financial coordination mechanisms, reforms in electricity, and a strategy to diversify oil export routes. But political announcements matter only if they become visible in services, stability, and household experience.

This is where the survey moves from leadership to delivery. Iraqis were asked to judge the government on security, electricity, higher education, water, basic education, and health, areas where success or failure is experienced directly rather than through political messaging.

The resulting picture is not uniform. Some areas perform better than others, reinforcing the idea that the first 100 days are better understood as an uneven opening phase than as a single verdict on the government.

04 · The economic test

For many Iraqis, government performance eventually becomes a household question.

Al-Zaidi inherited pressure around liquidity, public salaries, and an economy still heavily tied to oil. His government has pointed to financial reforms and efforts to diversify export routes, but the political value of those steps depends on whether citizens believe the broader economy is improving.

The survey captures both sides of that judgment: how Iraqis assess the government’s performance on the economy so far, and what they expect over the next 12 months. Forty-six percent expect the economic situation to improve, a meaningful sign of optimism, but not an unconditional vote of confidence.

05 · The corruption gap

Headline recoveries are one thing. Changing public perception is another.

The government says anti-corruption efforts have recovered $160 million, 380 billion Iraqi dinars, and 402 kilograms of gold. Those are the kinds of figures that can signal activity at the top of the state, but the harder question is whether Iraqis believe corruption itself is becoming less entrenched.

Here, the survey shows the scale of the challenge. Sixty percent say corruption exists in state institutions and agencies to a great extent. That makes anti-corruption performance a critical test of whether early government action can alter a much deeper public judgment.

06 · The road ahead

The government has political runway. The question is what it does with it.

One hundred days cannot settle the success or failure of a government. What they can reveal is the shape of its starting position: relatively strong personal approval for the prime minister, more uneven confidence across institutions and services, cautious economic optimism, and a corruption problem that remains deeply embedded in public perception.

Seventy-one percent think the al-Zaidi government will complete its constitutional four-year term. That is significant because it suggests that many Iraqis expect continuity. But continuity is not the same as confidence, and time in office is not the same as progress.

The first 100 days gave al-Zaidi a strong opening. The next test is whether that political honeymoon can be converted into visible, durable results.

Conclusion

The first 100 days are a starting point, not a final verdict.

The survey shows a government entering its next phase with an important advantage: many Iraqis are still willing to give it time. Al-Zaidi's personal standing is stronger than confidence in several institutions, service delivery remains uneven, economic optimism is cautious, and corruption continues to shape how people judge the state.

That gives the government a real opportunity, but also a clear standard. If early decisions become improvements people can see in electricity, water, schools, health, the economy, and accountability, the first 100 days may become the foundation for more durable trust. If those changes remain mostly at the level of announcements, early approval will become harder to sustain.

The opening mandate is there. The next phase will be judged on delivery.
